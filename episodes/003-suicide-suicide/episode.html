Album Review: Suicide by Suicide (1977)

In this episode, Dan and Carl dive into Rolling Stone's 498th best album of all time, Suicide by the band Suicide, released in 1977. Known for its pioneering proto-punk, proto-industrial, and proto-electronica sounds, this record sparks a conversation about its influential yet divisive nature.

Episode Highlights:

  • Intro and Background: The album, released by Red Star Records, was recorded in four days after the band practiced live for five years. Its original release isn't available on streaming platforms, but the 2019 remaster is.
  • Musical Influence: The hosts mention Suicide's impact on artists like Nine Inch Nails, Sonic Youth, and LCD Soundsystem, while also speculating on influence from Lou Reed and Elvis Presley.
  • Critical Opinions:
    • The hosts acknowledge the album's historical significance but are divided on its musical merit. Dan describes it as "proto-everything," while Carl finds the lack of progression in tracks a challenge.
    • They explore the tension between respecting Suicide's avant-garde approach and grappling with its abrasive sound.

Track-by-Track Breakdown:

  • Ghost Rider
    • Kicks off with repetitive drum machine and synth patterns. The initial excitement quickly fizzles as the song offers little variation.
  • Rocket USA
    • Described as a "drone of sound," this track also fails to offer progression. Dan notes a disturbing comparison to the sound of driving with the windows down.
  • Cheree
    • With over 8 million plays on Spotify, this is the most popular track on the album. Featuring a basic chord progression, Carl humorously calls it a "cut-rate Twist and Shout."
  • Johnny
    • The most blues-influenced track, though Dan derides it as "the most irritating 12-bar blues ever."
  • Girl
    • Built on a bossa nova rhythm, this song is deemed overly simplistic. Carl doubts the band even programmed the drum machine for this one, suspecting a preset rhythm was used.
  • Frankie Teardrop
    • The album’s most notorious track, clocking in at over 10 minutes. A story of murder-suicide told over sparse soundscapes, it is uncomfortable and difficult to listen to.
  • Che
    • Described as "pressure waves in your ears," this closing track is repetitive and forgettable, offering little to redeem the overall experience.

Artist Connections

  • The track "Frankie Teardrop" directly inspired Bruce Springsteen's "State Trooper", showcasing its reach into unexpected musical territories. - R.E.M. covered "Ghost Rider (YouTube)," though it remains an obscure part of their repertoire.

Final Thoughts:

  • Would You Hit It?: Dan gives a resounding NO, while Carl adds that recommending this album might end friendships.
  • Quality Time Segment: Dan compares this album to baking soda—a raw ingredient necessary for better music to follow, but tough to consume on its own.
